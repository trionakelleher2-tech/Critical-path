Words you will need

ActivityA job that takes time. Drawn as an arrow, labelled with its letter and its duration in weeks.
NodeA circle. A moment in time when the activities arriving at it are finished and the ones leaving it may start. Nodes take no time.
DurationHow long an activity takes, in weeks here.
ESTEarliest start time: the top box on a node. The soonest the activities leaving that node could begin.
LFTLatest finish time: the bottom box. The latest the activities arriving at that node may finish without delaying the whole project.
RouteAny chain of arrows from the start node to the final node.
Critical pathThe longest route. It sets how long the project takes. Any delay on it delays the finish.
FloatSpare time on an activity that is not on the critical path: how late it can run without delaying the finish.

Challenge brief

  1. Mission 1 · the overrun
    Recruitment (C) overruns by 3 weeks: set it to 7. Does the opening date move? Now set it to 8, then 9. Explain what happened at 9 using the word "float".
  2. Mission 2 · the switch
    Reset. Find the smallest change to a single duration that moves the critical path onto C–E. There is more than one answer; which would be cheapest for the business to cause, and which to fix?
  3. Mission 3 · the squeeze
    Reset and cut the deadline to 15 weeks. Which activities would you shorten, in what order, and what would each cost in money or quality? What is the shortest the project can ever be if only D is shortened?

Exam-style question and model points

Assess the value of critical path analysis to a business opening a new café. (12)

Points for: identifies the minimum project duration and the activities that cannot slip; shows where resources can be moved from activities with float to critical ones; supports just-in-time ordering of equipment and stock, improving cash flow; gives contractors and staff a shared, visible plan.

Points against: durations are estimates, and for a first café the business has no track record to estimate from; the network must be kept up to date or it misleads; it says nothing about cost or quality; managers may fixate on the critical path and neglect near-critical routes, which become critical after a small delay.

Judgement: highly valuable for a project with clear, sequenced tasks and an external deadline, but the output is only as good as the time estimates fed in, so it works best alongside contingency time and regular review.
